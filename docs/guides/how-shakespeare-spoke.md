# How Did Shakespeare Sound? Reconstructing Early Modern Stage Speech

*A survey of the evidence, the reconstruction, and what remains contested.*

## The short answer

If you could stand in the yard of the first Globe in 1601 and hear *Hamlet* spoken from the stage, the accent would strike a modern ear as none of the things people expect. It would not be the "posh," vowel-rounded Received Pronunciation of Gielgud or Olivier — that register is a nineteenth-century invention and an anachronism on the Jacobean stage. Nor would it be a recognizable modern regional accent. It would be **rhotic** (every written *r* sounded), faster and more clipped than the cathedral-cadence we associate with "classical" delivery, full of vowels caught mid-shift between their medieval and modern values, and saturated with puns and rhymes that have since fallen silent. Listeners who hear modern reconstructions tend to grasp at comparisons — Irish, West Country, Scots, American, Yorkshire, even stage-pirate — and the fact that no single label sticks is itself a clue to what the accent actually was.

The reconstruction now generally heard in performance is called **Original Pronunciation (OP)**, and it rests on roughly a century and a half of philological work, consolidated and popularized over the last two decades by the linguist David Crystal and the actor Ben Crystal.

## How we can possibly know

No recordings exist, obviously, and the period has no phonetic notation in the modern sense. Reconstruction is an inferential, probabilistic craft that triangulates several independent kinds of evidence. The strength of any single reconstructed sound depends on how many of these lines of evidence converge on it.

**1. The orthoepists and grammarians.** This is the backbone of the serious work. Across the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, a steady stream of spelling reformers, grammarians, and teachers tried to describe — sometimes with invented phonetic alphabets — how English was actually pronounced, precisely because the gap between spelling and speech was already becoming "bizarre." The key witnesses for Shakespeare's lifetime are John Hart (*An Orthographie*, 1569), William Bullokar, Alexander Gil (*Logonomia Anglica*, 1619), Robert Robinson (*The Art of Pronunciation*, 1617), and slightly later Christopher Cooper (1680s). Ben Jonson's posthumous *English Grammar* (1640) belongs here too: it famously describes the letter *r* as "the dog's letter," sounded with a snarl, and explicitly notes that it was pronounced at the ends of words — direct testimony from inside Shakespeare's own theatrical circle that London speech of the period was rhotic. As the standard summary has it, these grammarians' works form the backbone of every reconstruction of Early Modern English sound change.

**2. Rhyme.** Couplets and especially the *Sonnets* preserve pairings that no longer chime: *proved / loved*, *love / remove*. A lost rhyme suggests the two words once shared a vowel. This evidence must be handled with care, because poets also used eye-rhymes and inherited conventional rhymes, so a single dead rhyme proves little on its own — but a *pattern* of them, corroborated by the orthoepists, is strong.

**3. Puns and wordplay.** Homophones and near-homophones surface jokes that are invisible today. The standard examples: in the Prologue to *Romeo and Juliet*, *loins* and *lines* were near-identical; in *As You Like It* (2.7), Touchstone's "from hour to hour" turns on *hour*, *whore*, and *ore* sounding alike, which is why Jaques laughs; Falstaff's *reasons* play in *1 Henry IV* trades on *reason* and *raisin*. A great deal of the recovered wordplay is bawdy — much of it surfaces only in OP, which restores a layer of groundling-pleasing innuendo that RP buries.

**4. Spelling.** Before standardization, occasional, variant, and phonetic spellings — including those of the deliberate spelling reformers — leak information about sound. A scribe who writes a vowel "wrong" in a consistent direction is telling us something.

**5. Comparative and foreign evidence.** Manuals teaching English to French or other foreign speakers, and the way English rendered loanwords, let scholars peg "doubtful" English vowels against better-documented continental ones. German philologists before 1914 did much of this synthesis.

**6. Metre and scansion.** The verse itself encodes pronunciation. Syllable counts reveal where the *-ed* ending was sounded ("belovèd"), where *-ion* was disyllabic ([sɪ.ən], so "condition" filled four beats), and where word-stress fell differently than today (*revénue*, *aspéct*, *commendáble*).

**7. Typology of sound change.** Because we know the *direction* of well-understood changes — above all the Great Vowel Shift — we can place Shakespeare's English on the timeline between Chaucer's and our own, even where direct evidence is thin.

## What it actually sounded like

The single most important fact is that Shakespeare's English was caught **mid-Great-Vowel-Shift**. The long vowels had begun their long migration upward and forward but had not arrived at modern values. The result is an accent that is neither Middle English nor modern, with several systematic features:

### Consonants

- **Rhoticity.** Every written *r* was pronounced and coloured the vowel before it: *art*, *bird*, *for*, *here* all sounded their *r*. Postvocalic *r*-loss in southern English is an eighteenth-century development — *after* Shakespeare — so his London was firmly rhotic. This is the single feature that most distinguishes OP from modern RP and is the best-attested of all.
- **The *wh* / *w* distinction held.** *Which* and *witch*, *whales* and *Wales*, were not homophones; *wh-* carried a breathy [ʍ].
- **Final *-ing* was [ɪn].** "Running," "loving" ended in a plain *n*; the "dropped g" so often mocked as vulgar today was simply the norm.
- **Initial clusters in transition.** The *k* of *knight* / *know* and the *w* of *write* / *wrong* were weakening but may still have left a faint trace; *gnaw*-type *gn-* likewise.

### Vowels

Vowel reconstruction is the harder, more contested part, and exact qualities vary among scholars. The broad consensus picture for educated London speech around 1600, described here by Wells's lexical-set labels:

| Lexical set | Examples | Reconstructed value | Note vs. modern RP |
|---|---|---|---|
| FACE | name, take, day | [ɛː] (monophthong) | not the modern gliding [eɪ] |
| GOAT | home, go, stone | [oː] (monophthong) | not the modern [əʊ] |
| PRICE | time, mine, eye | [əɪ] (centred onset) | not yet [aɪ] |
| MOUTH | house, now, down | [əʊ] (centred onset) | not yet [aʊ] |
| GOOSE | boot, moon | [uː] | as now, but no glide |
| TRAP / BATH | cat, bath, path | [a] (front) | no TRAP–BATH split; *bath* short |
| STRUT / FOOT | cut, put | [ʊ]~[ə] | FOOT–STRUT split incipient: *cut* ≈ *put* |
| FLEECE | see, meet | [eː] raising toward [iː] | shift in progress |
| MEAT (ea-words) | sea, speak, great | [ɛː], distinct from FLEECE | the old MEAT–MEET distinction, since mostly merged |

Several consequences are worth drawing out. The absence of the **TRAP–BATH split** means *bath*, *path*, *grass* had a short front vowel with no "broad a." The incomplete **FOOT–STRUT split** means *cut* and *put*, *blood* and *good*, sat much closer together. And the still-live **MEAT–MEET distinction** — *meat* with [ɛː] against *meet* with [eː]/[iː] — is why a handful of words (*great*, *break*, *steak*) preserve the older value to this day while the rest of their set went one way and *meet* went the other.

### Rhythm and register

Beyond the segmental detail, OP changes the *feel* of the verse. Crystal and the actors who have worked in it consistently report that it runs faster, lands more conversationally, and foregrounds wordplay that modern delivery flattens. It also strips away the class connotations of RP: there is no "elevated" or "common" accent baked into OP, which lets the social dynamics of a scene be carried by the writing rather than by the actors' vowels.

## The "it sounds like ___" problem

Audiences hearing OP for the first time reliably reach for a comparison — Irish, West Country / Somerset, Scots, American, occasionally Yorkshire or Australian. The interesting point is that **they disagree**, and the disagreement is diagnostic rather than embarrassing. OP shares its rhoticity and several conservative vowels with each of those accents in part, because all of them preserve older features that RP discarded. It therefore activates a different listener's "that reminds me of…" reflex depending on which feature catches their ear. OP resembles none of these accents in full; it is its own thing, and the modern accent it most closely echoes is an accident of which conservatism a given listener happens to notice first.

## A century and a half of scholarship

The reconstruction is not new, even if the *performance* practice is. The lineage:

- **Alexander J. Ellis**, *On Early English Pronunciation* (1869–89) — the first great systematic attempt.
- **Henry Cecil Wyld**, *A History of Modern Colloquial English* (1920).
- **Helge Kökeritz**, *Shakespeare's Pronunciation* (1953) — a landmark, but built heavily on puns and rhyme and now criticized for over-asserting homophony where the evidence supports only nearness.
- **E. J. Dobson**, *English Pronunciation 1500–1700* (1957; 2nd ed. 1968) — the monumental, source-critical synthesis of the orthoepists; still the standard reference for the period's phonology.
- **Fausto Cercignani**, *Shakespeare's Works and Elizabethan Pronunciation* (1981) — a rigorous corrective focused on Shakespeare specifically.
- **Roger Lass**, phonology chapter in the *Cambridge History of the English Language*, vol. III (1999).
- **David Crystal**, *Pronouncing Shakespeare* (2005) and *The Oxford Dictionary of Original Shakespearean Pronunciation* (2016) — the current reference for the OP movement, the dictionary now cross-searchable online via Oxford Reference.

## Original Pronunciation as performance practice

The modern OP movement effectively began at Shakespeare's Globe in London. The reconstructed Globe had pursued "original practices" in costume, music, and staging since its 1990s rebuild, but long assumed that an OP production would alienate audiences. The opposite proved true: the **2004 OP *Romeo and Juliet*** (a handful of performances) and the **2005 *Troilus and Cressida***, with David Crystal as language consultant, drew enthusiastic responses and demand for more. Ben Crystal subsequently built a touring OP ensemble and an extensive body of teaching and performance work; the British Library issued a recorded OP anthology in conjunction with its *Evolving English* exhibition; and OP productions have since been mounted well beyond the Globe, including a *Hamlet* by the Classical Studio at NYU's Tisch School. The practice has also expanded past Shakespeare to other early authors, since the same methods apply to any pre-standardization text.

## What's genuinely "latest"

The field has not had a revolution — the core reconstruction has been stable for years, and the headline facts (rhoticity, mid-shift vowels, no TRAP–BATH split) are not in dispute. The recent movement is at the edges and in consolidation:

- **Reference consolidation.** Crystal's 2016 *Oxford Dictionary of Original Shakespearean Pronunciation*, with its online edition, is now the practical standard for anyone staging or studying OP, replacing the need to reason case-by-case from primary sources.
- **Digital corpora of the orthoepist record.** Recent quantitative work has begun treating the surviving grammarians and pronouncing dictionaries as a searchable dataset rather than a set of individually-interpreted texts — for example, database-driven studies of the development of English vowel systems across 1500–1800, and digital projects cataloguing eighteenth-century pronouncing dictionaries. These firm up the *timeline* of changes (and, by fixing when rhoticity was lost in London to the eighteenth century, reconfirm that Shakespeare's stage was rhotic).
- **Spread of performance practice.** The most visible recent development is simply that OP has moved from a Globe novelty to a teachable, documented technique used by companies and conservatories, with an expanding catalogue of recordings.

## Caveats worth keeping in view

OP is best understood as **a** plausible reconstructed accent for a metropolitan stage around 1600 — not **the** accent, and not a recording. Three limits matter especially:

1. **There was no single accent.** Elizabethan and Jacobean London was a churn of regional in-migration, social registers, and generational variation, all sitting on top of an in-progress vowel shift. OP smooths this into one performable compromise.
2. **Shakespeare's own speech ≠ stage OP.** He was a Warwickshire man, and a Stratford upbringing would have left Midlands features in his personal speech that a London stage standard would not reflect.
3. **The evidence underdetermines the fine detail.** Exact vowel qualities, allophonic variation, and intonation are reconstructed within ranges, and scholars have disagreed (Kökeritz vs. Dobson vs. Cercignani) on specific values. OP makes pragmatic choices to be both defensible and performable.

None of this undercuts the central, well-supported claim. The accent in which *Hamlet* was first spoken was rhotic, vowel-shifted in mid-stream, faster and earthier than the "classical" tradition trained us to expect, and rich in rhymes and puns we have been missing for four centuries.
